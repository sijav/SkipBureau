# Registering where you live in Germany

**Agreed 2026-09-12**, after three turns in `talk/germany/anmeldung.md`. The
draft put up for sign-off had **six** overclaims. Each sentence below is the
corrected one, and none of it may be softened, sharpened or given colour when it
becomes a guide.

## What a reader is told

**The rule, and it is the same everywhere.** Unless a statutory exemption or
exception applies, anyone who moves into a dwelling must register with the
Meldebehörde **within two weeks**[^bmg-17-two-weeks] of moving in. It is **§17(1)
Bundesmeldegesetz**, it is federal, and no Land can lengthen or shorten it. Your
nationality does not matter and neither does being an EU citizen. For a child
under sixteen[^bmg-17-under-sixteen], the person whose dwelling they move into registers them.

**Two weeks, not three months.**[^bmg-17-two-weeks] If you are moving to Germany to live and you
take a flat, you have two weeks[^bmg-17-two-weeks] from moving in. The three-month figure[^bmg-27-three-months] is real
but it describes other situations: a temporary stay by somebody who otherwise
lives abroad and is not registered in Germany, where registration falls due
within two weeks[^bmg-27-two-weeks] after the stay passes three months[^bmg-27-three-months] (**§27(2)**); and a stay in
a hotel or similar by somebody with no registered German dwelling, on the same
pattern (**§29(1)**). The temporary-stay exception under §27(2) does not apply
to asylum seekers or other foreign nationals who move temporarily into a
reception facility or other assigned accommodation (**§27(3)**).

**If you miss it.** Failing to register on time, deliberately or carelessly, can
be fined **up to €1,000**[^bmg-54-fine-late-registration] (§54(2)(1), (3)). That is a ceiling, not a standard
charge. The **€50,000**[^bmg-54-fine-fictitious-address][^bmg-19-6-fictitious-address] figure you may read about is for offering somebody a
**fictitious registration address** and has nothing to do with being late.

**The landlord confirmation, and what to do without one.** The person who
provides your accommodation, which for a subtenant is the main tenant rather
than the owner, must confirm your move within two weeks[^bmg-19-confirmation-deadline][^bmg-17-two-weeks][^berlin-landlord-confirmation-two-weeks], either in a signed
written confirmation or electronically **directly to the Meldebehörde through
the procedure under §19(4)**. It must carry their name and address, the owner's
name if that is somebody else, the date you actually moved in, the address, and
the names of everyone registering.

If they refuse or are late you must tell the registration office **without
delay**[^bmg-19-without-delay] (§19(2)), and federal guidance says the registration **must go ahead
anyway** if you really did move in, with the confirmation supplied afterwards[^bmgvwv-confirmation-afterwards]. A
lease is not a substitute for it. A provider who refuses, delays or confirms
falsely can also be fined up to €1,000[^bmg-54-fine-confirmation].

**What it costs, and this is where Germany differs from itself.** Registration
is free in Berlin[^berlin-registration-free], Munich[^munich-registration-free], Düsseldorf[^duesseldorf-registration-free], Wiesbaden[^wiesbaden-registration-free], Freiburg[^freiburg-registration-free] and under Saxony's
state guidance[^saxony-registration-free]. **Hamburg charges €16**[^hh-registration-fee-16], set by its own fee ordinance and raised
to that figure on 1 January 2026[^hh-fee-amendment-2026]. The charge is for one person **or for a family
who share both the address they left and the address they are arriving at**[^hh-tariff-2015-item-2], so
a qualifying family pays €16 once[^hh-registration-fee-16], not each. We could not establish which
relationships count as a family for that tariff, so do not assume it covers
housemates or relatives arriving separately. Hamburg's fee comes from Hamburg's
**general fee legislation** rather than from the federal registration law, which
is why it can exist at all while the two-week deadline[^bmg-17-two-weeks] cannot vary.

**What registering gets you.** Your **tax identification number** arrives on its
own after your first registration[^bzst-id-at-first-registration] and is yours for life[^bzst-for-life]; if no letter comes
within three months[^bzst-three-months] the Federal Central Tax Office has a procedure for asking
again. A **bank account** is a separate application and, as a matter of law,
registration is not a precondition: the financial regulator expressly includes
people with no fixed residence among those who may be entitled to a basic
account. **Health insurance** is also separate and registering does not enrol you
in health insurance. Official guidance for newcomers expects you to be covered
from the day you arrive[^miig-cover-from-arrival], so do not wait for your Anmeldung to arrange it.

**Cities differ in their registration procedures.** Berlin registers by
appointment, in person or through an authorised representative, and its online
route is for moves within Germany rather than a first arrival from abroad.
Munich also allows a representative and publishes a form for a missing landlord
confirmation. Hamburg's page for arrivals from abroad says everyone moving in
must attend in person, which sits awkwardly with federal guidance allowing
representation, and we could not find a Hamburg rule that overrides it. The
service pages we checked for Berlin, Munich and Hamburg do not offer ordinary
registration by post.
